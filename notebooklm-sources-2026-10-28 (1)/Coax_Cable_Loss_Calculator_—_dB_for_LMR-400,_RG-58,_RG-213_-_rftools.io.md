## Assumptions

6 items

01 Attenuation per 100 ft is k₁· √f + k₂· f with f in MHz, a conductor (skin-effect) term and a dielectric term; dividing by 0.3048 gives dB/100 m, and the loss scales linearly with length, Loss = α· L/100.

02 Times Microwave's LMR cables use the k₁ and k₂ printed on each datasheet (VSWR 1.0, +25 °C); Belden's and CommScope's (Andrew) sheets give only a table, so their k₁ and k₂ are least-squares fits to it, and the CommScope tables are at 20 °C.

03 Where a fit departs from its table by more than 10 % (RG-58 and RG-316 below 10 MHz, RG-174 and Belden 9913 below 50 MHz, RG-6 below 67.5 MHz), or the frequency is above a cable's rated maximum or the last frequency its table gives, the loss is still computed, with a warning naming the limit.

04 The loss is the matched insertion loss of new cable at the datasheet's temperature: there is no correction for temperature, ageing or moisture, and no connector or VSWR mismatch loss.

05 Power out is the input power less the loss, P_out = P_in − Loss in dBm, into a matched load; the power and voltage delivered are 10^(−Loss/10) and 10^(−Loss/20) of the input.

06 Impedance is listed, not modelled: the 75 Ω RG-6 shows its loss in a 75 Ω system, and a 75 Ω cable in a 50 Ω system adds a mismatch loss this does not compute.

## How it works

[Coax](https://rftools.io/calculators/rf/coax-loss/) loss [calculator](https://rftools.io/calculators/rf/coax-loss/) computes signal power loss for any cable type, run length, and frequency — RF engineers, broadcast system designers, and antenna installers use this to calculate link budgets and select appropriate cable for their frequency range. Total loss combines conductor loss (proportional to sqrt(f) due to skin effect) and dielectric loss (proportional to f due to dielectric heating): alpha_total = alpha_c * sqrt(f) + alpha_d * f, the form of the manufacturers' k1 * sqrt(f) + k2 * f coefficients and of transmission line theory in Pozar's 'Microwave Engineering' (4th ed.).

At HF frequencies (3-30 MHz), conductor loss dominates — a 100m run of RG-58 loses 6.5 dB at 30 MHz versus 3.7 dB at 10 MHz. Above 1 GHz, dielectric loss becomes significant: solid polyethylene (er = 2.25, tan_delta = 0.0002) adds 2.7 dB/100m at 1 GHz while PTFE (er = 2.1, tan_delta = 0.0001) adds only 1.3 dB/100m (Pozar: alpha_d = pi * f * sqrt(er) * tan_delta / c nepers per metre). Low-loss cables like LMR-400 use foamed polyethylene (er = 1.5, tan_delta = 0.0001) achieving 13.5 dB/100m at 1 GHz versus 47.8 dB/100m for RG-58.

Learn Physics

Velocity factor VF = 1/sqrt(er) directly correlates with loss: foam dielectrics (VF = 0.85) have 30-50% lower loss than solid PE (VF = 0.66) at the same frequency because the electromagnetic field travels through more air. Temperature increases loss by approximately 0.2%/C for copper conductors due to increased resistivity.

## Practical tips

Select cable where total run loss is < 3 dB for transmit systems (50% power efficiency) and < 1 dB for receive systems where every dB affects noise figure directly

For runs over 30m at UHF (400+ MHz), upgrade from RG-58/RG-8X to LMR-400 or equivalent — the 3-4x cost difference is justified by 2.6-3.3x lower loss (in dB at 435 MHz)

Use hardline (7/8" or 1-5/8") for permanent installations over 50m at cellular/microwave frequencies — Andrew LDF5-50A (7/8") loses 3.9 dB/100m at 900 MHz versus 12.8 dB for LMR-400

## Common mistakes

Using room-temperature specifications for outdoor installations — cable loss increases 0.2%/C; at 60C ambient, add 8% to datasheet values. A 10 dB link margin can shrink to 9.2 dB on a hot day

Ignoring connector losses in link budget — each N connector adds 0.1-0.15 dB at 1 GHz, SMA adds 0.15-0.2 dB, and PL-259 (UHF) adds 0.3-0.5 dB. Four connectors in a typical installation add 0.5-1.0 dB

Not accounting for VSWR mismatch loss on top of cable loss — a 2:1 VSWR adds 0.51 dB mismatch loss; with cable loss, this compounds: 3 dB cable loss + 2:1 VSWR = 3.51 dB total, not 3 dB

Comparing cables using different length units — always normalize to dB/100m or dB/100ft; LMR-400 at 4.69 dB/100m sounds better than 1.43 dB/100ft until you realize they're the same cable

## Frequently asked questions

4 questions

LMR-400 vs RG-213: when is the upgrade worth it?

At HF (< 30 MHz), both cables lose under 3.5 dB/100m (2.2 and 3.2 dB at 30 MHz) — the upgrade provides minimal benefit. At VHF/UHF, the difference is dramatic: at 450 MHz, LMR-400 is 8.9 dB/100m versus RG-213 at 15.5 dB/100m. For a 30m run, LMR-400 loses 2.7 dB while RG-213 loses 4.7 dB — that 2.0 dB difference is 1.6 times the power at the antenna. Rule of thumb: upgrade when frequency * length (MHz * meters) exceeds 10,000.

How do I account for connector losses?

Add per-connector loss based on frequency and connector type. At 1 GHz: N-type 0.1 dB, SMA 0.15 dB, BNC 0.2 dB, PL-259/SO-239 (UHF) 0.3-0.5 dB. Losses increase with frequency — at 6 GHz, N-type is 0.2 dB and SMA is 0.25 dB. For precision measurements, mated pair loss should be verified per IEEE 287-2007. Worn or contaminated connectors can add 0.5+ dB — inspect and clean before critical operations.

What cable for a 2.4 GHz WiFi antenna extension?

At 2.4 GHz, losses are high for all cables. LMR-200: 54 dB/100m (keep runs under 2m for < 1.1 dB loss). LMR-400: 21.7 dB/100m (acceptable to 5m). LMR-600: 14.2 dB/100m (acceptable to 8m). For runs over 8m, use LMR-900 (9.6 dB/100m) or install the access point closer to the antenna. At 5.5 GHz, losses are about 1.6 times higher — cut the distances above to about 60%.

How does cable loss affect receiver sensitivity?

Cable loss between antenna and LNA adds directly to system noise figure: NF_system = NF_cable + NF_LNA. A 3 dB cable loss before the LNA degrades sensitivity by 3 dB (halves detectable signal power). For receive-critical applications (radio astronomy, weak-signal amateur, GPS), mount the LNA at the antenna feedpoint and run DC power up the coax. A 0.5 dB LNA with 20 dB of gain at the antenna, followed by 10 dB of cable loss, yields F = 1.122 + (10 - 1)/100 = 1.212, an NF_system of 0.84 dB. The same LNA after the cable yields NF_system = 10 + 0.5 = 10.5 dB.
