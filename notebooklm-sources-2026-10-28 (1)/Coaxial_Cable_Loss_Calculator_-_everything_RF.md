# Coaxial Cable Loss Calculator

Work out how much signal a coaxial cable run loses, and how much power actually reaches the far end. Enter a cross-section and the calculator derives the attenuation from the conductor and dielectric losses, or pick one of 31 cable types from RG-58 to 1-5/8 in Heliax, or type in two attenuation figures from any datasheet. It gives you the loss of your run in dB, the attenuation per 100 m and per 100 ft at your frequency, the power and efficiency at the far end, the extra loss a mismatched load adds, and charts of loss against both frequency and length.

## What this calculator works out

Every metre of coaxial cable between your radio and your antenna turns some of your signal into heat. This tool tells you how much: the attenuation of the cable at your frequency, the loss of the whole run at your length, the power that actually arrives at the far end, and how much worse a mismatched load makes it. It also plots loss against frequency and against length, so you can see where the run stops being acceptable rather than only whether it is acceptable today.

You can describe the cable in three ways, and all three feed the same arithmetic. Enter the cross-section if you are designing the cable or all you have is a set of dimensions, which is the tab that opens first. Pick a type from the library if you know the part number. Type in two published attenuation figures if you have a datasheet for something the library does not carry.

## How coaxial cable loses signal

Two mechanisms, and they behave differently with frequency. That difference is the whole reason a cable that is fine at 145 MHz can be useless at 5.8 GHz.

**Conductor loss**: current flowing along the centre conductor and back along the shield meets resistance, and that resistance rises with frequency because of the [skin effect](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-is-skin-effect). At DC the current uses the whole cross-section of the conductor. At RF it crowds into a surface layer one skin depth thick, and that depth shrinks as 1/√f. Half the copper stops carrying current, then three quarters of it, and the effective resistance climbs as √f. Our [Skin Depth Calculator](https://www.everythingrf.com/rf-calculators/skin-depth-calculator) will give you the actual figure for a given metal and frequency; at 1 GHz in copper it is about 2.1 µm, which is why the surface finish of a conductor matters more than the metal underneath it.

**Dielectric loss**: the insulator between the two conductors is a capacitor being charged and discharged at the signal frequency, and no real insulator does that for free. The fraction of stored energy lost per cycle is the loss tangent, tan δ, and because there are f cycles every second this term rises in direct proportion to frequency. The material's [dielectric constant](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-is-dielectric-constant) sets the velocity factor at the same time, which is why foamed and air-spaced dielectrics are both faster and lower loss than solid ones.

Add the two and you get the form every cable manufacturer publishes:

α ( f ) = K 1 f + K 2 f

with f in MHz and α in dB per 100 ft. K 1 is the conductor term and K 2 is the dielectric term. Times Microwave prints exactly this equation with its coefficients on the LMR datasheets, so for those cables this calculator uses the manufacturer's own numbers rather than a fit.

α(f) K₁√f K₂ f Frequency f Attenuation

The two loss terms and their sum. The conductor term dominates at low frequency and grows slowly; the dielectric term starts almost invisible and grows in a straight line, so it takes over at the top of a cable's range. Symbols only, no scale.

The practical consequence: at HF and VHF you buy lower loss by buying a physically larger cable, because the conductor term falls as the conductors get bigger. At microwave frequencies you buy it by changing the dielectric, because a bigger cable does nothing about tan δ.

### Where the two-term model stops working

Below a certain frequency the centre conductor is not yet fully in the skin-effect regime, attenuation flattens towards its DC value, and the √f term underestimates it. The effect is largest on thin cables with plated-steel centre conductors: the Belden datasheet for [RG174 coaxial cables](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-are-rg174-coaxial-cables) gives 1.9 dB/100 ft at 1 MHz where the fitted two-term model gives 0.75. Each library entry therefore records the frequency window its coefficients were built from, the chart shades everything outside that window, and the calculator tells you when you have left it. It still gives you a number, because an honest extrapolation with a warning is more useful than a blank box, but it says which it is.

## The three ways to describe your cable

Pᵢₙ Pₒᵤₜ Source Load α(f), Z₀ ℓ L = α(f) × ℓ, Γ set by the load impedance

The quantities the calculator works with, whichever tab you use: an attenuation per unit length α(f), a length ℓ, an input power, and a load whose impedance sets the reflection coefficient Γ. Proportional, not to scale.

**Cable from the library**: thirty-one types grouped by family, from [RG58 coaxial cables](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-are-rg58-coaxial-cables) and 0.086 in semi-rigid up to 1-5/8 in corrugated Heliax, each with the datasheet it came from named on screen. Inside each group the list runs from the lossiest cable to the least lossy, which is also roughly smallest to largest. Where a manufacturer publishes K 1 and K 2, those are used unchanged. Where only an attenuation table is published, the two coefficients come from a weighted least-squares fit to that table, and the panel tells you how closely the fit tracks it. Treat these as typical figures, not guarantees: an RG number is a specification for dimensions and materials, not for loss, so the same [RG213 coaxial cables](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-are-rg213-coaxial-cables) from two makers can differ by twenty per cent. The [RF Coaxial Cable Specifications](https://www.everythingrf.com/tech-resources/rf-cable-specifications) tables are worth a look if you want to compare types side by side.

**Datasheet figures**: give the tool two attenuation points from any datasheet and it solves for K 1 and K 2 exactly. Choose the two points as far apart as the datasheet allows and both well inside the RF range, because two points close together leave the split between the terms badly conditioned. If the solution comes back with a negative coefficient the tool says so: that means the two figures you gave are not consistent with conductor loss plus dielectric loss, which usually means one of them is below the skin-effect knee or one is a typo. Some datasheets print the coefficients directly, and there is a mode for that too.

## Working from the cross-section

The third tab computes everything from first principles, which is what you want when you are choosing dimensions rather than a part number.

d D Inner conductor Dielectric Shield Jacket σ : conductor conductivity εᵣ : dielectric constant tan δ : loss tangent Z₀ = (59.96/√εᵣ) ln(D/d)

The five quantities a coaxial line's electrical behaviour depends on. Everything else follows from these. Symbols only, no dimensions.

Characteristic impedance comes from the diameter ratio and the dielectric constant alone:

Z 0 = 59.96 ε r ln ( D d )

The same relationship, solved the other way round, is what our [Coaxial Cable Impedance Calculator](https://www.everythingrf.com/rf-calculators/coaxial-cable-calculator) does. Conductor attenuation then follows from the surface resistivity of the metal and the two circumferences the current flows on, and dielectric attenuation from tan δ and the phase constant. The general form of that quantity, for any transmission line, is what the [Attenuation Constant (α) Calculator](https://www.everythingrf.com/rf-calculators/attenuation-constant-calculator) handles.

Two honest caveats about the physical model. First, it assumes smooth solid cylinders. Real cables are stranded, braided or corrugated, and their surfaces are rough on the scale of a skin depth, so measured conductor loss sits above the theoretical figure. Checking LMR-400 against its own datasheet: the smooth copper calculation gives 3.53 dB/100 ft of conductor loss at 1 GHz where the manufacturer's K 1 √f term gives 3.87, so that cable runs ten per cent above theory. Five to twenty per cent is the usual range, and the roughness factor under Advanced options is there for you to apply it. Second, the model assumes a pure TEM wave. Above the TE 11 cutoff, which the results panel reports and which our [Coaxial Cable Cutoff Frequency Calculator](https://www.everythingrf.com/rf-calculators/coaxial-cable-cutoff-frequency-calculator) gives directly, the cable can carry a waveguide mode as well and none of this applies. That is the reason large cables have low frequency limits: 7/8 in Heliax has beautifully low loss and stops at about 5 GHz.

## Reading the results

**Matched cable loss**: the loss of the cable alone into a perfectly matched load. This is what a datasheet quotes and what you should use when comparing cables. It is the same quantity as the [insertion loss](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-is-insertion-loss) of the assembly, minus the connectors.

**Total loss with mismatch**: what the run actually costs you with the load you have. This is the number behind the power and efficiency results.

**Power at the far end and efficiency**: the same statement in watts and in per cent. Decibels compress badly at the top end, so the percentage is often the more persuasive figure in a design review. Use the [dBm to watts Conversion Calculator](https://www.everythingrf.com/rf-calculators/dbm-to-watts) and the [Watts to dBm Conversion Calculator](https://www.everythingrf.com/rf-calculators/watt-to-dbm) if you need to move between the two elsewhere in your budget.

## Why a mismatched load makes a lossy cable worse

Power reflected by the load travels back down the cable and is attenuated on the way, then some of it reflects again at the source and travels forward, and so on. The energy that ends up dissipated in the cable rather than in the load is the extra loss the mismatch causes. The standard result, with a the matched power-loss ratio and |Γ L| the magnitude of the load reflection coefficient, is:

L total = 10 log a 2 − | Γ L | 2 a ( 1 − | Γ L | 2 )

Two things fall out of it that are worth internalising. On a nearly lossless line a mismatch costs almost nothing in total loss, because the reflected power gets another chance. On a lossy line it costs a lot, because every round trip is taxed. A load VSWR of 3 adds 0.29 dB to a 0.5 dB run and 0.97 dB to a 3 dB run, and the gap keeps widening from there.

The second is the trap the calculator makes visible in the results panel. The VSWR you measure at the shack end of a long, lossy feeder is always better than the VSWR at the antenna, because the reflection is attenuated twice on its round trip. A 3 dB feeder turns a load VSWR of 5 into an input VSWR of 2.0. A meter reading 2.0 looks like a serviceable antenna and is not one. If you want to convert between the various ways of stating the same thing, see [What is VSWR?](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-is-vswr), [What is Return Loss?](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-is-return-loss) and [VSWR vs Return Loss](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/vswr-vs-return-loss), or run the numbers through the [VSWR to Return Loss Calculator](https://www.everythingrf.com/rf-calculators/vswr-to-return-loss-calculator). The separate question of how much power never enters the load at all is [mismatch loss](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-is-mismatc-loss-ml), reported here as well, and its effect on a gain measurement is what the [Gain Uncertainty due to Mismatch Calculator](https://www.everythingrf.com/rf-calculators/gain-uncertainty-due-to-mismatch) quantifies.

Ranked on loss alone the order is obvious, and it is also the order of cost, diameter and minimum bend radius, so loss alone is not the criterion. The criteria that actually decide it are: keep more than about a fifth of the power, so the run costs you under 7 dB; stay with a cable a person can route by hand and terminate with ordinary tools; and use connectors you can buy on a Tuesday. **On those three, LMR-400 is the recommendation**: 6.5 dB, 22 per cent through, 10.3 mm outside diameter, and N-type connectors everywhere. The two larger cables buy 2 to 3 dB more, which is worth having if the run is fixed and already installed in a duct, and not worth having if anyone has to bend it round a corner. Below LMR-400 the numbers stop being a trade and start being a mistake: LMR-240 throws away 94 per cent of the transmitted power, and the RG-58A/U figure is an extrapolation above that cable's data window in any case, which the tool flags. Nobody should be planning a 2.4 GHz run on RG-58 when [LMR 400 coaxial cables](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-are-lmr-400-coaxial-cables) cost a fraction of the power they save.

## Temperature, and the other things that move the number

Copper resistivity rises about 0.393 per cent per degree, and conductor loss follows its square root, so a cable on a hot roof at 85 °C loses roughly 12 per cent more than its 20 °C datasheet figure. Twelve per cent of 6 dB is 0.7 dB, which is enough to matter on a marginal link and is exposed under Advanced options. Datasheet figures are quoted at 20 °C, and this calculator uses that as its reference.

Other things that shift real cable loss away from any model: water in the jacket, which is catastrophic and permanent; a shield that has been flexed past its rated bend radius; connectors, which typically cost 0.1 to 0.3 dB each at microwave frequencies and are not counted here; and, on cheap cable, plain manufacturing spread. If your measured loss is 15 per cent above the datasheet, suspect the cable and the connectors before you suspect the arithmetic. Shielding quality does not show up in attenuation at all but decides whether the run is quiet, and [Shielding Effectiveness in RF Cables and Cable Assemblies](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/shielding-effectiveness-in-rf-cables-and-cable-assemblies) covers that side of it.

## What this calculator does not model

**Connector loss**: add 0.1 to 0.3 dB per connector at microwave frequencies, more for a worn or poorly terminated one.

**Power handling and thermal derating**: a cable can be electrically fine and still cook. That needs the cable's thermal resistance, the ambient temperature and the duty cycle.

**Phase and delay**: velocity factor is reported, but electrical length, delay and phase stability over temperature are separate questions. [The Cable Is the Measurement: Rethinking Phase Stability in RF Test](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/the-cable-is-the-measurement-rethinking-phase-stability-in-rf-test) is the place to start, and the [Time Domain Reflectometry (TDR) Length Calculator](https://www.everythingrf.com/rf-calculators/time-domain-reflectometry-length-calculator) turns a delay measurement into a length.

**Passive intermodulation**: see [What is Passive Intermodulation (PIM)?](https://www.everythingrf.com/community/what-is-pim-passive-inter-modulation). It is a connector and workmanship problem, not an attenuation one.

**Frequency dependence of tan δ**: treated as constant, which is a good approximation for PE and PTFE across a decade or two and a poor one across five.

**Ageing, moisture and corrosion**: no model survives a cracked jacket.

**Manufacturing spread**: wider than every model error above put together for generic RG types.
